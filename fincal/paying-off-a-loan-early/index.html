Paying off a loan early: shorter term or lower payment, and is the fee worth it?

Nanoko Studio · Updated 21 Nov 2026

You owe 200,000 at 6% a year with 20 years to go. You pay off 20,000 early with a 2% fee:

Shorter term39,694interest saved, debt-free 41 months sooner
Lower payment14,448interest saved, 143 less every month
After the fee, the 20,000 earns≈ 5.8% a yearclose to the 6% loan rate

A bonus, an inheritance or a savings account that has just matured: putting it towards the mortgage is a natural thought. Before you do, two questions are worth answering: does the early repayment fee eat the benefit, and when the lender asks whether you want a shorter term or a lower monthly payment, which should you pick?

Example: 200,000 owed with 20 years left

A mortgage with equal monthly payments of 1,433, at 6% a year, with 240 months left. You pay 20,000 early and the lender charges 2% on it, which is 400. Then you choose one of two options:

  • Shorter term: keep paying 1,433 a month as before, so the loan ends sooner.
  • Lower payment: keep the 20 years and spread the remaining 180,000 over 240 months, so each payment drops to 1,290.

A shorter term saves more, but is not necessarily better

The table shows the shorter term saving 39,694, well over twice the 14,448 saved by lowering the payment. That is not because it is the smarter option. With a shorter term you keep paying the full amount every month, so you put more money into the loan over the following years. More money into the loan means less interest, of course.

A fairer measure is the return: the yearly percentage of interest that the money paid early lets you avoid. Without a fee, both options return exactly the loan rate, 6% a year. With a 2% fee, the shorter term returns 5.9% a year and the lower payment 5.8% a year, almost the same.

So choose based on your cash flow:

  • Steady income and you want to be debt-free sooner: shorter term.
  • You want breathing room each month in case income drops: lower payment. If you later put the monthly difference towards the loan, you end up close to the shorter-term result anyway.

How big a fee is too big?

Paying early is like putting money into a risk-free savings account that pays your loan rate. The fee is paid once, up front, so the longer the loan still has to run, the smaller the fee is per year. The table below shows the return after the fee on a 6% loan, using the lower-payment option:

With 5 years or more left, a 1-3% fee only pulls the return down to 4.8-5.8% a year. With 1 year left it is a different story: a 3% fee leaves just 0.5% a year, often less than a savings account pays. If the fee drops or ends soon under your contract, waiting a few months can pay off.

Compare this return with what the money could earn if you kept it, for example in savings. If paying early returns more, it is the better use. On a variable-rate loan the real return follows the rate in the years ahead, so the higher rates go, the more paying early is worth.

What to ask your lender first

  • How the fee works: a percentage of the amount paid early or of the whole balance, and how it changes over the years.
  • Free allowance: many lenders let you pay a set amount each year, often around 10% of the balance, without any fee.
  • Whether you can choose a shorter term or a lower payment, or the lender applies one by default.
  • Ask for the new repayment schedule afterwards and check it against the calculator above.

Do not use your whole emergency fund. Money paid into a loan is hard to get back when you need it, so keep at least a few months of expenses aside.

Frequently asked questions

How much is a typical early repayment fee?
Often around 1-3% of the amount paid early, falling as the loan gets older, and many loans charge nothing after a few years or below a yearly allowance. It differs by lender and product, so check the early repayment section of your contract.
Should I pay off my loan early or save the money?
Compare the return after the fee with the interest your savings would earn. If paying early returns more, it is the better choice, as long as you still keep enough for emergencies.
Is it worth paying early during a fixed-rate deal?
During the deal, money paid early avoids the fixed rate, and afterwards it avoids whatever rate follows. Fees are usually highest during the fixed period, so many people wait until it ends, or stay within the free allowance.
Shorter term or lower payment: which is better?
The return is almost the same. A shorter term saves more interest because you keep paying more each month. Choose a lower payment if you want an easier monthly budget.
