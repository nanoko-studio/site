Fixed deposit interest: take it monthly or at maturity?

Nanoko Studio · Updated 21 Nov 2026

Put 50,000 in a 12-month fixed deposit, assuming 4.5% a year paid at maturity or 4.4% a year paid monthly:

Total if paid monthly and saved2,230monthly, each payment saved at 3%

When you open a fixed deposit or term deposit, the bank may ask whether you want the interest paid at maturity or every month. The monthly option usually comes with a slightly lower rate. So does getting the money sooner make up for the lower rate?

Why the monthly rate is lower

With interest at maturity, the bank keeps all the interest until the end of the term. With monthly interest you get the money earlier and can save or spend it, so the bank trims the rate to account for that. If the bank prices it fairly, both options end up close, and the difference comes down to what you do with the monthly payments.

Example: 50,000 for 12 months

The rates here are assumptions for comparison, not any bank's offer. At 4.5% a year paid at maturity, you get 2,250 in one go after 12 months. At 4.4% a year paid monthly, you get 183 a month, or 2,200 over the year.

If you move each monthly payment into a savings account paying 3% a year, it earns another 31, for a total of 2,230. Interest at maturity is still ahead by 20.

The monthly line climbs steadily while the maturity line stays at zero until month 12 and then jumps. At the end of the term the maturity line is still slightly higher.

Leaving the interest in a current account that pays nothing is the worst choice, 50 behind. In this example, even earning the same 4.5% on the monthly payments still leaves you slightly behind, because the monthly rate was cut by more than the benefit of getting paid early. On 50,000 the gap is small, much smaller than the difference between rates at different banks.

What monthly rate breaks even

To check whether the bank's monthly rate is fair, work out the break-even rate: the monthly rate at which saving each payment at 3% a year ends up exactly equal to interest at maturity. If the bank offers more than this, monthly interest wins.

TermInterest at 4.5%Lowest monthly rate to break even
6 months1,1254.47% a year
12 months2,2504.44% a year
24 months4,5004.37% a year
36 months6,7504.31% a year

The longer the term, the lower the break-even rate, because interest at maturity is not compounded during the term while monthly payments have longer to earn. On 2-3 year deposits, monthly interest that you save regularly can come out ahead if the bank's discount is small. If the bank offers the same rate for both options, monthly interest that you save again always comes out slightly ahead.

When monthly interest makes sense

  • You need a regular income, for example in retirement. Giving up a little interest for a steady monthly payment is a fair trade.
  • Long terms where you will reliably save the interest, if the offered monthly rate is above the break-even rate in the table.
  • If you don't need the money, interest at maturity is usually simpler and slightly better. Roll over the principal and interest together and you get compound interest. See how savings grow over many years →

One caution: breaking a fixed deposit early usually costs you interest. With monthly payouts, interest already paid above what you are entitled to may be taken back from your principal, so monthly interest does not protect you if you withdraw early.

Frequently asked questions

Is it better to get fixed deposit interest monthly or at maturity?
Interest at maturity usually comes out slightly ahead, because the monthly rate is set a little lower. With 50,000 for 12 months at 4.5% at maturity or 4.4% monthly, maturity wins by about 20 if you save the monthly interest at 3%, and by about 50 if it sits in a current account.
Why is the monthly interest rate lower?
Because you receive the interest earlier and can use or save it. The bank lowers the rate to balance that benefit.
Who should choose monthly interest?
People who need a regular income, such as retirees. If you don't need the money, interest at maturity rolled over with the principal is usually better.
What if the bank offers the same rate for both?
Then monthly interest wins, as long as you save each payment somewhere that pays interest. The earlier money earns a little extra.
